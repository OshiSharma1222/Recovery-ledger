The problem

A payment fails. The service was delivered. The money never arrives.

In India this happens constantly, because UPI Autopay and e‑mandates fail in ways cards do not. Mandates expire. Amount caps get breached. Customers revoke permission silently, and the merchant only learns when the debit bounces.

Almost every billing system answers the same way: retry on day one, day three, day five. The reason for the failure is never read, so the same three attempts are spent on a customer who is simply between paychecks and on one who cancelled their mandate two months ago.

What the merchant actually receives

payment_cancelled

One short string from the bank. That is the entire input.

What the merchant already holds

mandate cap, expiry, status

They registered the mandate. Almost no dunning system joins this to the retry decision.

The insight

A quarter of the money cannot be recovered by anyone, ever.

Failures are not alike, and the difference is not a matter of degree. It is the difference between a delay and a wall.

Low balance · a timing problem

The customer is between paychecks. Retry just after payday and the money is usually there. Retry two days before and it is close to a coin flip.

Expired mandate · a wall

The permission to charge is gone. The bank will refuse every attempt, forever. No retry schedule, however clever, can recover a single rupee of it.

26% of this ledger is unrecoverable by construction. A fixed schedule spends three attempts on every one of those rows, every cycle, and recovers nothing. That is the case for treating recovery as resource allocation, where the hard part is knowing what not to chase.

How it works

Four steps, and one of them is stopping.

Detect

payment_cancelled

A debit bounces or a chargeback lands. Either becomes one row: amount, customer, and the raw bank code exactly as received.

Diagnose

MANDATE_REVOKED

The code is ambiguous on its own. Joined with the merchant's own mandate record it resolves to a real cause, at 96.3% accuracy.

Decide

retry_at

Each cause has one action that can work: retry at a learned moment, renew the mandate, split a debit that breached its cap.

Stop

ABANDON

When nothing can work, the engine spends nothing and the saved attempts go where money can actually come back.

Customer contact is capped at two messages per case. Recovery is not allowed to become spam, and that cap costs about one percent of recovery to buy it.

The comparison that matters

Timing is necessary. It is not sufficient.

Commercial dunning products already optimise when to retry, so beating a naive schedule proves little. I built that competitor out of my own parts: a policy using the identical learned timing model, picking the best possible moment every time, but never asking why the payment failed.

The second lane

Chargebacks are the same problem, so they use the same ledger.

Money that arrived and was taken back is still money the merchant earned and does not have. Disputes flow through the identical schema and statuses, with a different action set. Adding them required no change to the ledger at all, which is the architecture claim demonstrated rather than asserted.

A real case: ₹1,299 under Visa 13.2

The cardholder says they cancelled before the charge. Visa requires proof of when the cancellation was requested, and support handled it by phone and never logged it. Without that document the case cannot be reviewed at all.

Recommendation: do not contest. Filing costs ₹850 to lose. The useful output is not the score, it is the pattern: fix the missing record upstream and these stop being losses.

Honesty, and what is next

The limitation, stated first.

The customers are simulated and I authored that world, so this measures policy quality against a stated model, not production reality.

What protects the result: the simulator's parameters were frozen and committed before a single line of the policy engine existed, verifiable in one command of git history. The ranking holds across twenty seeds the code has never seen. And the benchmark warns if any policy beats the oracle, which caught a real bug in my own favour before it reached the README.

What is real: the Razorpay error codes, the RBI e‑mandate constraints, and the Visa and Mastercard dispute codes.

  • Verify the RBI figures against the primary circular, not secondary reporting.
  • Learn the ambiguous code mappings from data instead of ruling them.
  • Respect per‑rail re‑presentment caps as a policy constraint.
  • Add a third money source, such as refund abuse, to prove the ledger extends again.
